I've been building products on the side since university: a campus forum in 2017, Play Store apps in 2019, a sports-prediction app that crossed 100K downloads, then a string of AI startups with friends from 2024 on. Almost all of them *worked*. Almost none became a business.

This is the honest version of what I learned.

## 1. "Build, then sell" is the default. It's also the trap.

Engineers can build anything in a weekend, so we do: NeosBuy (an AI shopping assistant) and ResumeCrafter (AI job applications) were both fully working before we'd talked to ten real customers. Then we went looking for users, and then for investors.

The order should be reversed. **Sell first, then build.** A waitlist, a pre-order, a signed letter of intent, a manual "concierge" version you run by hand. Anything that proves someone will pay before you write the code.

## 2. Measure the funnel, not the feelings

A founder friend made us write down three numbers: **visitor → user → customer**. We made AI-generated Reels and Shorts, spent $5–10 at a time on ads, and watched the funnel. It was humbling, and it was the most useful thing we did all year, because it replaced "people seem to like it" with a number.

## 3. A non-technical co-founder is not a silver bullet

After two technical-only startups we thought the missing piece was a business co-founder. We found great people through YC Startup School and learned a lot from them about GTM, investor pipelines and operations. But distribution doesn't come with a co-founder. Someone has to own the problem of *finding customers*, and that someone can be you.

## 4. Watch the unit economics early

One project had real revenue from day one. It also had high operating costs and thin margins in a crowded market. Revenue isn't the same as a business. Look at margins before you fall in love.

## 5. Platforms are landlords

App stores rejected one of our apps over a duplicate category, then pulled it over a trademark we didn't know existed, even though we owned the domain. Then I spent six months trying to open a US bank account for an LLC. **Own your distribution** (email list, website, community) and check trademarks *before* launch.

## 6. Time is the real constraint

All of this happened alongside a demanding full-time job, a master's degree and, recently, a wedding. Spreading thin across several projects at once meant none of them got the focus it needed. **One thing at a time.**

## What's next

I'm not done. But the next one starts with a customer, not a codebase.

*If you're a technical founder stuck in the same loop, write to me. I'd love to compare notes.*
